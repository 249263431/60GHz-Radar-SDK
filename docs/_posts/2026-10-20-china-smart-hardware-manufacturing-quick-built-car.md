You cannot help but notice it: China's smart-hardware manufacturing is extraordinarily strong — strong enough that most ordinary users can no longer tell a good product from a bad one. That is both a testament to raw manufacturing capability and the seed of a new industry problem. When the hardware barrier is pulled low enough, the difference between products stops being "can it be built at all" and becomes "how accurate, how reliable, how durable."

## It started with a smart ring

The trigger was a colleague forwarding me several competitors' sleep-tracking smart rings. Overseas users broadly have a preventive-health habit, so this category has real demand abroad. The competitor claims a deep-sleep error of only about three minutes a night, and a light-sleep error of roughly seventeen. I looked at it myself — genuinely impressive. Whether those numbers are true is impossible to verify from the outside, but three minutes across an eight-hour night is already more accurate than a lot of medical devices.

The price contrast is the part that sticks. An Oura ring sells for several hundred US dollars. A near-identical product from Huaqiangbei costs under 100 RMB. Same function, same form factor, an order of magnitude apart in price.

## The "quick-built car" path: get it running, then tune

The typical China smart-ring route goes like this: a chip vendor ships a reference solution, someone opens an iron-ring mold, stuffs a PCB inside, and it measures. Whether it is actually accurate usually takes a great deal of user-side secondary development to grind into shape. That is roughly the "quick-built car" idea — throw it together so it drives, then optimize step by step.

It is no different from Bluetooth earphones. We use Apple's, where battery life and build quality are assured. Huaqiangbei turns out visually identical units for tens of RMB. The manufacturing ability really is that strong, and that is the double edge: the same capability that lets a good product exist also lets a barely-tuned one ship.

## Millimeter-wave radar is at this stage too

Our own mmWave radar sits at this same stage. This is simply the current state of Chinese manufacturing — yet I think the "quick-built car" concept touches every industry. Some manufacturers take a chip-vendor solution, slap together a demo — say, a fall-alarm — and, much like the university instructors I described earlier, go sell it the moment the function works. The phenomenon is real.

But we have since fixed a great deal of it. The move that matters is from "the function can be implemented" to "it can be delivered stably" — from a demo that proves a point to a product that keeps working in a real home, night after night.

## Tiered consumption is fair; positioning is the key

Look at the buyer side and the picture is reasonable: some people buy cheap, some pursue quality, and every tier has a legitimate market. So what decides a company is positioning. If you chase the market wherever it swings, you drift toward a race to the bottom — I uninstalled Pinduoduo years ago and simply will not use that style; I would rather buy elsewhere. The same logic applies to hardware.

For a company selling hardware solutions, competing in the low-price trench is a losing game. It is better to make accuracy, reliability, and long-term service genuinely solid, and serve the customers who actually care about quality.

| Posture | "Quick-built car" | "Stable delivery" |
|---|---|---|
| Primary goal | Function works, ship now | Accurate, reliable, durable |
| What differentiates | Can it be built | How well it is built |
| Typical buyer | Price-first | Quality-first |
| The risk | Eroded trust, returns | Narrower but loyal base |

*Honest caveat: these are impressions from forwarded samples and field observation, not a controlled benchmark; the Oura and Huaqiangbei price points are illustrative of a well-known gap, not a quote of the day.*

## Takeaway

- China's manufacturing strength is real — strong enough that users often cannot tell good from bad hardware.
- When the hardware barrier is low, competition moves from "can it be built" to "how accurate, reliable, and durable."
- The "quick-built car" pattern — chip solution, mold, PCB, ship, then tune — is how a lot of consumer hardware reaches the market fast.
- Our mmWave radar was at that stage too; the real progress is from "function implemented" to "delivered stably."
- Tiered demand is legitimate; the differentiator is positioning. Competing on price alone is a race to the bottom — build accuracy and service instead.
